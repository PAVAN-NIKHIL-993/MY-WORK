### What actually happens  
An ADC pin (like on entity["microcontroller","ESP32","Espressif microcontroller family"] or NodeMCU) is **very high impedance**. That’s engineer-speak for “it barely draws any current.”

- Typical input leakage current: **~1 µA or less** (often even in nanoamps)
- That’s **0.000001 A**  
- Your battery won’t even notice. It’s like losing a drop of water in the ocean.

### But here’s the catch (because life isn’t that easy)
If you connect the battery **directly** to the ADC pin:
- You risk **overvoltage damage** (ESP ADC max ≈ 3.3V)
- You *don’t* control measurement stability

So you *should* use a voltage divider.

### Voltage divider current (this is what actually matters)
Let’s say you used:
- R1 = 4.7kΩ  
- R2 = 18kΩ  

Then current =  
\[
I = \frac{V}{R1 + R2}
\]

So for a 4.2V battery:
\[
I ≈ \frac{4.2}{22700} ≈ 0.185 \text{ mA}
\]

👉 That’s **185 µA**  
Still tiny… but now *noticeable compared to ADC leakage*.

### Translation into human terms
- ADC pin itself: basically **nothing**
- Voltage divider: **this is your real drain**

### Pro move (since you’re not here to play beginner games)
If you want *almost zero drain*:
- Use **higher resistors** (e.g., 100k + 470k)
- Or switch the divider using a MOSFET/transistor
- Or only enable it during measurement

### Bottom line
- Direct ADC draw → negligible  
- Divider draw → small but real  
- Poor design → fried ESP, and then you get to buy another one
