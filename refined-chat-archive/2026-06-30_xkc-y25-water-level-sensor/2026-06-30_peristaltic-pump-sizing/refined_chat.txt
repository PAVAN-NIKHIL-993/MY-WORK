You have two ways to get **600 mL/min** from a peristaltic pump:

1. **Increase the pump RPM** (simplest).
2. **Reduce the tube compression circumference** (smaller pump head or smaller rotor), which changes the volume moved per revolution. From your question, you're aiming for the second.

Here's the catch, because physics continues to be stubborn despite humanity's repeated attempts to negotiate with it.

The volume pumped per revolution is approximately:

\[
V = A \times L
\]

where:
- **A** = inside cross-sectional area of the tube
- **L** = length of tube compressed during one revolution (roughly the rotor circumference)

For your tube:

- ID = **5 mm**
- Radius = **2.5 mm**

Cross-sectional area:

\[
A = \pi r^2 = \pi(2.5)^2 \approx 19.6\ \text{mm}^2
\]

So every **1 mm** of compressed tube moves approximately

\[
19.6\ \text{mm}^3 = 0.0196\ \text{mL}
\]

### Required circumference

If your motor speed is known,

\[
L=\frac{600}{0.0196\times RPM}
\]

Examples:

| Pump RPM | Required Tube Length (Circumference) |
|---------:|-------------------------------------:|
| 50 RPM | 612 mm |
| 100 RPM | 306 mm |
| 150 RPM | 204 mm |
| 200 RPM | 153 mm |
| 300 RPM | 102 mm |
| 600 RPM | 51 mm |

The corresponding rotor diameter is

\[
D=\frac{L}{\pi}
\]

Examples:

| RPM | Rotor Diameter |
|----:|---------------:|
|100|97 mm|
|150|65 mm|
|200|49 mm|
|300|32 mm|

### Important limitation

A **5 mm ID silicone tube is quite large** for a peristaltic pump.

If you make the rotor **too small**:
- the tube bends too sharply,
- the tube won't fully recover,
- flow becomes inaccurate,
- tube life drops dramatically,
- the rollers may not fully occlude the tube.

A good rule is:

- Minimum bend radius ≈ **5 to 8 × tube OD**
- Smaller than this rapidly damages silicone tubing.

### If you tell me:

- **Motor RPM**
- **Tube OD (outside diameter)**
- **Number of rollers (2, 3, or 4)**

I can calculate:
- the **exact rotor diameter**,
- **roller diameter**,
- **roller spacing**,
- **occlusion amount**,
- and predict the actual flow rate within a few percent. That's much more reliable than guessing from circumference alone, because peristaltic pumps are surprisingly fussy little machines that spend their lives squeezing rubber in circles and somehow still demand precision.

Let's derive it from first principles. Peristaltic pumps are wonderfully simple: every full revolution pushes forward approximately the volume of liquid that occupied the length of tube squeezed during that revolution. Reality then deducts its usual "engineering tax."

## Step 1: Calculate the tube's cross-sectional area

Your tube has:

- **Inside Diameter (ID) = 5 mm**
- Radius = 5/2 = **2.5 mm**

Area of a circle:

\[
A=\pi r^2
\]

Substituting:

\[
A=\pi\times(2.5)^2
\]

\[
A=3.1416\times6.25
\]

\[
A=19.63\ \text{mm}^2
\]

This means every **1 mm** length of tubing contains:

\[
19.63\ \text{mm}^3
\]

Since:

- **1000 mm³ = 1 mL**

every millimetre of tube contains

\[
\frac{19.63}{1000}=0.01963\ \text{mL}
\]

---

## Step 2: Calculate the rotor circumference

You want a **40 mm rotor**.

Circumference is

\[
C=\pi D
\]

So

\[
C=3.1416\times40
\]

\[
C=125.66\ \text{mm}
\]

One revolution moves the compression point around approximately **125.66 mm** of tubing.

---

## Step 3: Calculate the liquid displaced

Each millimetre contains

\[
0.01963\ \text{mL}
\]

Multiply by the tube length compressed:

\[
V=125.66\times0.01963
\]

\[
V=2.47\ \text{mL}
\]

So:

> **One complete revolution theoretically pumps about 2.47 mL.**

= **247 mL/min**

≈ **600 mL/min**

## Why is this only theoretical?

Imagine a syringe.

If the plunger moves exactly 10 mm, you get exactly the expected volume.

A peristaltic pump is not a syringe.

The silicone tube:
- stretches,
- compresses,
- rebounds,
- slips slightly,
- never seals perfectly.

So the actual flow is

\[
\text{Actual Flow}=
\text{Theoretical Flow}
\times
\eta
\]

where **η** (volumetric efficiency) is typically:

- 0.80 to 0.95

For example, at 243 RPM:

Theoretical:

600 mL/min

Actual (90% efficient):

540 mL/min

So you'd increase the speed to around **270 RPM** to compensate.

---

## One important correction

The calculation above assumes the **entire rotor circumference contributes to pumping**, which is **not strictly true**.

A real peristaltic pump has:
- 2 or 3 rollers,
- only one section of tube fully trapped at a time,
- inlet and outlet transition zones,
- some tube expansion after the roller passes.

Because of this, the **actual displaced volume per revolution is usually about 85-95% of the simple circumference calculation**, even before accounting for elastic losses.

For engineering design, this simplified formula is excellent for estimating rotor size and motor speed. Once the pump is built, you calibrate it by measuring the actual output over one minute and adjusting the RPM. That's how commercial peristaltic pumps achieve accurate dosing despite all the rubbery mischief happening inside.
