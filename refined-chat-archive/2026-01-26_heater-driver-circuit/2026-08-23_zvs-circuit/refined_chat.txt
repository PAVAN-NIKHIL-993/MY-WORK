If by **“500J / 800J”** you mean capacitor markings, there is an important little trap here, because capacitor manufacturers apparently decided humans needed another code system to suffer through.

### Capacitor marking conversion

The common 3-digit code works like this:

| Marking | Capacitance | Equivalent |
|---|---:|---:|
| **104J** | 100 nF | **0.10 µF** |
| **154J** | 150 nF | **0.15 µF** |
| **204J** | 200 nF | **0.20 µF** |
| **224J** | 220 nF | **0.22 µF** |
| **304J** | 300 nF | **0.30 µF** |
| **334J** | 330 nF | **0.33 µF** |

**J = ±5% tolerance.**

So the capacitors shown in your second circuit:

**0.33 µF × 2 = 0.66 µF total**  
assuming they are connected in parallel as shown.

### But “500J” and “800J” need caution

If you literally mean:

- **500J** → under the standard 3-digit pF code, that's **50 pF**, NOT 500 nF.
- **800J** → **80 pF**, NOT 800 nF.

If the seller is calling a capacitor **500 nF / 0.5 µF** or **800 nF / 0.8 µF**, that's a different notation and you should give me the exact marking/photo.

## What happens if you use 0.5 µF or 0.8 µF?

This is the important part for your **12 V induction heater**.

Your second circuit is a resonant tank. Roughly:

\[
f=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}
\]

So when you **increase C**, the resonant frequency **decreases**.

For example, compared with **0.66 µF**:

- **0.5 µF** → resonance frequency increases by about **15%**
- **0.8 µF** → resonance frequency decreases by about **9%**
- **1.0 µF** → resonance frequency decreases by about **19%**

But frequency isn't the only issue.

### The bigger problem is circulating current

At resonance, the capacitor and induction coil can have **very large circulating currents**, even though your supply is only 12 V.

Increasing capacitance changes the tank impedance and can substantially alter:

- MOSFET current
- capacitor RMS current
- coil current
- heating power
- MOSFET temperature
- switching losses
- resonance frequency
- ZVS behavior

So simply thinking:

> “Bigger capacitor = more heating”

is unfortunately not how this little copper-and-silicon circus works.

For the **IRFZ44N ZVS induction heater**, the capacitor bank is normally chosen together with the **coil inductance**. You should use **polypropylene film capacitors designed for high-frequency pulse/resonant service**, not ordinary electrolytics.

And **630 V** on the 0.33 µF capacitors in your image is the voltage rating, not the operating voltage. The capacitor can still experience substantial **AC RMS current**, which is often the real limitation in an induction heater.

If you're trying to replace the **0.33 µF × 2** capacitors in that exact circuit, tell me the **exact marking printed on your available capacitor** (or upload a close-up), and I can calculate whether it is a sensible replacement.

Yes, **you can target low input power**, but don't use “higher frequency = lower heating” as the control rule. In a resonant induction heater, that relationship is not reliable. Humans built resonance specifically to make simple relationships annoying.

For your target:

\[
V_{IN}=12V,\qquad I_{IN}\le1A
\]

you want approximately:

\[
P_{IN}\le12W
\]

### If your goal is gentle heating

For the circuit in your image, I'd approach it like this:

- Keep the **12 V supply**
- Set a **hard 1 A current limit**
- Use a **small work coil**
- Use a relatively **small resonant capacitor**
- Monitor MOSFET and capacitor temperature
- Don't deliberately push the circuit far away from resonance

The important point is that **changing capacitance changes the resonant frequency**, but it doesn't give you a predictable power limiter.

For example, if your existing tank is:

**0.33 µF × 2 = 0.66 µF**

then moving to **0.5 µF** or **0.8 µF** changes the resonant frequency, but the input current can still become quite high depending on the coil and load.

### For a guaranteed 12 V / 1 A ceiling

I'd use a **12 V current-limited supply** rather than trying to make the LC tank itself perform the current limiting.

Something like:

```text
12 V DC
   │
   │
 [1 A current limit]
   │
   ├───────────────┐
   │               │
   │       ZVS induction heater
   │               │
   └───────────────┘
```

Then the heater can attempt to draw more current, but the supply limits it.

**However:** some cheap current-limited supplies will collapse into hiccup mode when the ZVS circuit starts. A proper CC/CV supply with enough transient response is preferable.

### One more important thing

If you want **12 V × 1 A = 12 W maximum**, the common IRFZ44N ZVS induction-heater circuit is honestly a rather oversized architecture for the job. Those circuits are normally designed to exploit **large circulating resonant currents**, and they can draw several amps even from a 12 V source.

For a controlled ~10–12 W heater, I'd rather design the tank around your required heating target instead of trying to tame the 100+ W behavior of the classic ZVS board.

If you give me your **coil dimensions** (inner diameter, length, number of turns, copper-wire diameter) and the **capacitor value you intend to use**, I can calculate the approximate resonant frequency and choose the LC values for a **12 V, ~1 A maximum design**.

Not necessarily. **In your ZVS induction-heater circuit, higher frequency does not automatically mean lower heating.** The annoying bit is that several things change together.

For the same workpiece and coil:

- **Higher frequency** → shallower skin depth → heating is concentrated closer to the surface.
- **Lower frequency** → deeper penetration → heating can reach further into the metal.
- But the **actual heating power** depends heavily on coil current, tank resonance, coupling, MOSFET losses, and the workpiece.

For your circuit:

\[
f=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}
\]

So:

**Increase C → lower frequency**

**Decrease C → higher frequency**

If you change from your approximately **0.66 µF** tank to **0.5 µF**, frequency rises. That does **not** inherently mean less heating. If the ZVS circuit remains properly resonant and coil current stays high, heating can actually be **higher**.

### Practical rule for your 12 V / IRFZ44N heater

| Change | Resonant frequency | What tends to happen |
|---|---:|---|
| Smaller C | ↑ | Shallower heating, potentially higher losses/current |
| Larger C | ↓ | Deeper heating, potentially higher tank current |
| Wrong C | — | Poor ZVS, hot MOSFETs, weak heating |

For **steel**, frequency selection matters because skin depth approximately follows:

\[
\delta \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt f}
\]

So if you double the frequency, penetration depth becomes roughly **71%** of what it was.

**But don't choose the capacitor purely by “higher frequency = better.”** In this particular ZVS topology, getting the resonant point and circulating current right is much more important.

If you're targeting **maximum heating from your 12 V 10 A supply**, the coil inductance + capacitor value + MOSFET current need to be designed as one tank, rather than randomly swapping 0.33/0.5/0.8 µF capacitors and hoping the electrons develop a business strategy.
