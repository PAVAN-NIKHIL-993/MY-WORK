### Suggestions & Next Steps

- Power the ESP32 from a clean 3.3 V rail: ≥100 µF bulk + 100 nF ceramic at the VDD pins, and a separate regulator for servos/motors — brownouts masquerade as random firmware bugs.
- Respect boot strapping pins (GPIO 0/2/12 on classic ESP32, GPIO 2/8/9 on C3): no external pulls that fight the straps, and never drive GPIO12 high at reset on 5 V-tolerant modules.

That's an **MCP2515 CAN Bus Module** with a **TJA1050 CAN transceiver**.

image_group{"query":["MCP2515 CAN bus module TJA1050","CAN bus network automotive","CAN bus wiring diagram","ESP32 MCP2515 connection"]}

### What it does

The MCP2515 lets microcontrollers like:

- Arduino Uno
- ESP32
- STM32
- Raspberry Pi

communicate over a **CAN (Controller Area Network) bus**.

Think of CAN as a very robust industrial communication network where dozens of devices share the same two wires and exchange messages without chaos. Humans somehow managed to make a communication system that works reliably inside a car full of electrical noise. A rare engineering victory.

### Common Uses

#### 🚗 Automotive
Read data from:
- Engine ECU
- ABS module
- Dashboard
- Battery Management System (EVs)

Examples:
- RPM
- Vehicle speed
- Coolant temperature
- Fault codes

#### 🏭 Industrial Automation
- PLC communication
- Motor controllers
- Sensor networks

#### 🤖 Robotics
- Multiple motor drivers
- Distributed sensors
- Robot arm joints

#### 🔋 Battery Systems
- BMS communication
- Solar inverter networks

---

### Main Parts on the Module

**MCP2515**
- CAN controller
- Communicates with MCU using SPI

**TJA1050**
- CAN transceiver
- Converts logic signals to CANH/CANL bus signals

**Crystal (usually 8 MHz)**
- Clock source for MCP2515

**120 Ω Resistor**
- Bus termination resistor
- Usually enabled by jumper

---

### Connections

Module → ESP32

| MCP2515 | ESP32 |
|----------|--------|
| VCC | 5V |
| GND | GND |
| CS | Any GPIO |
| SCK | GPIO18 |
| SI (MOSI) | GPIO23 |
| SO (MISO) | GPIO19 |
| INT | Any GPIO |

CAN side:

| Terminal |
|-----------|
| CANH |
| CANL |

These connect to another CAN device.

---

### Important

A single module **cannot do anything by itself**.

You need:
- At least **2 CAN nodes**
- CANH connected together
- CANL connected together

Example:

```
ESP32 + MCP2515  <----CAN---->  ESP32 + MCP2515
```

Then they can exchange messages.

---

### Maximum Speed

Typical:
- 125 kbps
- 250 kbps
- 500 kbps
- 1 Mbps

Cable length decreases as speed increases.

---

### If your goal is Arduino/ESP32 projects

You can use it for:
- ESP32 ↔ ESP32 communication over long distances
- Reading CAN data from vehicles
- Building industrial sensor networks
- Robot communication systems

For simple Arduino-to-Arduino communication, UART, I²C, or RS485 is usually easier. CAN becomes useful when you need **noise immunity, reliability, and multiple devices on one bus**.

If you're talking about **reading CAN data from a vehicle**, the connection is usually:

```text
Vehicle CAN Bus          MCP2515 Module
---------------------------------------
CAN High (CANH)   --->   CANH
CAN Low  (CANL)   --->   CANL
```

Then connect the MCP2515 to your ESP32:

```text
MCP2515      ESP32
----------------------
VCC    --->  5V
GND    --->  GND
CS     --->  GPIO5
SCK    --->  GPIO18
SI     --->  GPIO23
SO     --->  GPIO19
INT    --->  GPIO4
```

### Finding CANH and CANL in a car

Most modern vehicles expose CAN on the **OBD-II connector**:

```text
OBD-II Pin 6  = CAN High
OBD-II Pin 14 = CAN Low
OBD-II Pin 4  = GND
OBD-II Pin 5  = GND
```

```text
OBD Pin 6  ---> MCP2515 CANH
OBD Pin 14 ---> MCP2515 CANL
```

### Important warning

Not every vehicle uses the same CAN speed.

Common bitrates:

- 125 kbps
- 250 kbps
- 500 kbps (very common)
- 1 Mbps

If the speed is wrong, you'll receive nothing. Because apparently making every manufacturer agree on one setting would have been too much peace and harmony for the automotive industry.

### Before connecting

Measure with a multimeter:

Between CANH and CANL on a powered-down vehicle:

- Around **60 Ω** → normal terminated CAN bus
- Around **120 Ω** → one terminator present
- Open circuit → probably not the correct wires

### What you can read

Depending on the vehicle:

- Engine RPM
- Vehicle speed
- Coolant temperature
- Throttle position
- Battery voltage
- Diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs)

For standard OBD-II PIDs you'll usually need an **OBD-II CAN protocol library** and send request frames, not just passively listen.

A **GaN charger** is a charger that uses **Gallium Nitride (GaN)** transistors instead of traditional silicon transistors.

image_group{"query":["GaN charger 65W","GaN charger internal components","USB C GaN charger","compact GaN charger comparison"]}

### Why GaN is special

Normal chargers switch power at high frequency using silicon MOSFETs.

GaN transistors can:
- Switch much faster
- Have lower losses
- Generate less heat
- Be physically smaller

So a 65W GaN charger can be smaller than an old 20W laptop charger. Humanity spent decades making chargers bulky bricks, then GaN came along and asked, "what if we just didn't do that?"

### Benefits

✅ Smaller size

✅ Higher efficiency (often 90-95%+)

✅ Less heat

✅ Higher power density

✅ Fast charging support

---

### Common Power Ratings

- 20W (phones)
- 30W
- 45W
- 65W (laptops + phones)
- 100W
- 140W+

---

### Inside a GaN Charger

Typical blocks:

```text
AC Mains
   │
EMI Filter
   │
Bridge Rectifier
   │
High Voltage Capacitor
   │
GaN Power Stage
   │
Transformer
   │
Synchronous Rectifier
   │
USB-C PD Controller
   │
Output
```

The GaN FET is usually on the primary side switching hundreds of volts at tens or hundreds of kHz.

### For Electronics Hobbyists

If you open one, you'll find:
- GaN FETs
- USB-PD controller ICs
- Optocouplers
- Flyback transformer
- SMD components packed incredibly tightly

Not a beginner repair project. Many parts sit directly on the 325V DC bus after rectification, which is quite enthusiastic about reminding people why isolation matters.

### Can you use one for ESP32/Arduino?

Absolutely.

A USB-C GaN charger can power:
- ESP32
- Arduino
- Raspberry Pi
- Development boards

through a suitable USB cable or USB-PD trigger module if you need 9V, 12V, 15V, or 20V outputs.

If you're asking because you want to **build a GaN charger**, that's a very different beast from normal SMPS design. The control, layout, EMI, and safety requirements get serious very quickly.

The **AMS1117** is a very common **linear voltage regulator**.

image_group{"query":["AMS1117 SOT223 package","AMS1117 3.3V regulator module","AMS1117 pinout","AMS1117 on ESP8266 module"]}

### What it does

It converts a higher DC voltage into a lower regulated voltage.

Examples:

- 5V → 3.3V
- 12V → 5V
- 9V → 3.3V

Common versions:

| Part | Output |
|--------|--------|
| AMS1117-3.3 | 3.3V fixed |
| AMS1117-5.0 | 5V fixed |
| AMS1117-1.8 | 1.8V fixed |
| AMS1117-ADJ | Adjustable |

---

### Pinout (SOT-223)

Front view:

```text
      _______
     |       |
GND  |       |  VOUT
VIN  |_______|

```

More precisely:

```text
Pin 1 = GND / ADJ
Pin 2 = VOUT
Pin 3 = VIN

Tab = VOUT
```

---

### Example: 5V → 3.3V

```text
5V ---- VIN   AMS1117-3.3   VOUT ---- 3.3V
GND --- GND                GND
```

Add capacitors:

```text
10uF
VIN ---||--- GND

10uF
VOUT --||--- GND
```

---

### Current Capability

Typically up to:

```text
800mA to 1A
```

But that's only with good cooling.

---

### Biggest Problem: Heat

Linear regulators burn excess voltage as heat.

Formula:

genui{"math_block_widget_always_prefetch_v2":{"content":"P=(V_{in}-V_{out})I"}}

Example:

```text
12V → 3.3V
Current = 500mA

Power loss =
(12 - 3.3) × 0.5

= 4.35W
```

4.35W is a lot for that tiny package. It will get hot enough to make you reconsider your life choices.

---

### Where You Find It

Commonly used on:

- ESP8266 NodeMCU boards
- Some ESP32 boards
- Arduino shields
- Cheap sensor modules
- LCD modules

---

### Dropout Voltage

AMS1117 needs about **1.1V to 1.3V** more on the input than the output.

For AMS1117-3.3:

- Input must be at least ~4.5V
- 3.7V Li-ion battery → **not suitable**
- 5V USB → works fine

---

### For ESP32

Good:

```text
5V USB → AMS1117-3.3 → ESP32
```

Bad:

```text
12V → AMS1117-3.3 → ESP32
```

because most of the power becomes heat.

For 12V supplies, use a **buck converter (MP1584, LM2596, MP2307, etc.)** first, then regulate if needed.

The AMS1117 is one of those chips that appears on half the electronics modules sold online. Tiny, cheap, reliable enough, and constantly being asked to dissipate far more heat than its package would prefer.
